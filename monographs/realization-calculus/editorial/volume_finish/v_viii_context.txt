1 How to read the formal companion
Chapter V places several kinds of mathematics in one signal processing language: coordinate parti-
tions, bounded Hilbert space operators, finite filter banks, finite tree optimization, formal series, and
projective actions. The formal development keeps their types and hypotheses distinct. Following those
distinctions is the most useful way to read its proofs.
A pointwise partition identity needs little structure. An energy theorem needs a normed space with
the intended norm. A transform theorem needs convergence. An optimal finite pruning needs a finite
family and an objective. A fractional linear expression needs a chart, while its projective action survives
at the chart’s poles. Each transition has a named construction or theorem in the source.
Follow the model before the theorem name
Start with the ambient type, then locate the bridge to the intended signal model. A coordinate
identity, a bounded operator theorem, and an analytic transform law have different inputs. The
exact excerpt shows where those inputs enter the declaration.

Reading this chapter
This chapter studies transfer semigroups in three complementary settings. Part I establishes the finite
formal core. Part II develops bounded refinement towers, probabilistic conditioning and analytical
realizations. Part III gives additional results on product-topology limits, reversible coarse dynamics,
filtering, perturbation and operator substitution. Their hypotheses distinguish the constructions even
where the underlying identities overlap.
The formal companion covers exactly the thirty-three scoped results of Part I. Its 341 supporting Lean
theorem declarations lie in 47 proof modules. The wider namespace audit includes definitions and
generated declarations. Formal coverage does not automatically extend to Parts II or III. The companion
supplies the exact targets, assumptions and specialization boundaries.
Part I includes three explicitly open conjectures. Part II supplies bounded or finite theorems for sev-
eral related questions. It does not settle arbitrary continuum limits, unbounded-generator convergence,
general statistical sufficiency, or globally optimal nonnormal model reduction. The reader should keep

Overview
This chapter develops the transfer-semigroup realization of the projector-resolved seed calculus.
A seed is propagated by a one-parameter semigroup, a Markov kernel, a normalized Bayesian trans-
fer, a fractional spectral power, a Green operator, or a potential-weighted Feynman–Kac operator.
Exact packet projectors resolve every transfer into input-output blocks, and the semigroup law be-
comes block convolution over intermediate packets. Coarse and fine generators are compared by a
refinement defect. A Duhamel identity proves that this generator-level defect is exactly the source
of the corresponding semigroup defect and gives a quantitative stability bound. In the stochastic
realization, point-mass seeds generate random walks and continuous-time Markov evolution. Ex-
act lumpability is the vanishing of the same refinement defect. Conditional expectation is treated
as a weighted orthogonal packet projector, with nested partitions producing martingale-difference
packets. Bayesian conditioning is a normalized nonlinear operator. Its independent-data composi-
tion law, packet-evidence decomposition, stability bound, conjugate updates, and continuous likeli-
hood flow are proved explicitly. The Bayesian refinement conjecture is resolved in finite dimensions:
for strictly positive likelihoods and at least two coarse packets, exact commutation for every prior
is equivalent to blockwise-constant likelihoods up to global scale, and every failure factors through
an explicit information-loss packet. Fractional powers are constructed packetwise through the spec-
tral projectors of a positive operator, and the square-root semigroup is recovered by subordination.
The fractional-naturality conjecture is also resolved for bounded nonnegative self-adjoint refinement
systems: the fractional intertwining defect has an exact resolvent integral, a gap-free Hölder cer-
tificate, a sharper common-gap estimate, and a Duhamel semigroup bound. Green kernels, finite-
difference Poisson problems, and second-order low-mode convergence provide the PDE realization.
A finite-state Feynman–Kac formula is proved by path enumeration. Exact Schur–Feshbach elim-
ination shows that discarded latent packets return as a frequency-dependent self-energy and, in
time coordinates, as a memory kernel. Survival and hazard functions are generated by transient
semigroups. Nineteen code-generated figures, machine-readable results, the original thirty-seven
automated tests, and seventeen added boundary-case checks accompany this reviewed edition. The

results in Appendix A. Finite-dimensional theorems, classical infinite-dimensional extensions, nu-

The central computational result concerns compilation. Suppose a finite rooted tree records candi-
date refinements. Each node may be kept as one representation or split into its children. If cost and error
are additive across the selected leaves, the optimal pruning is found exactly by dynamic programming.
A quantized error budget has a second exact dynamic program. The decision is stable when the score
gap exceeds the perturbation in the local models. A change inside one subtree requires recomputa-
tion only along the ancestor chain. These results prove a restricted packet compiler. They do not solve
unrestricted discovery or arbitrary device scheduling.
The measured part uses a sanitized corpus collected on one recorded CPU and one NVIDIA GeForce
RTX 3070 Ti Laptop GPU. The preserved corpus contains 154 recorded rows from sixteen suites. The
timings are inherited observations. No GPU timing is rerun for this edition. Regular equal block opera-
tors and block diagonal plus low rank operators show large gains. Recursive HODLR, Strassen multipli-
cation, iterative refinement, a small Walsh–Hadamard transform, and an attempted two stream overlap
provide negative controls. These rows are retained because structure does not guarantee speed.
The article separates four sources of gain:
representation,

kernel,

A single speedup number can mix all four. The benchmark protocol therefore requires case identity,
output contract, timing scope, source precision, correctness reference, discovery time, compilation time,
and reuse count. The archived rows do not satisfy every field of this ideal contract: source-value hashes
and explicit correctness-reference fields are absent, while setup, discovery, and reuse metadata are
sparse. Recorded ratios are consequently descriptive comparisons under their stated scopes, rather than
fair matches with complete provenance.
The mathematical statements are finite. The empirical statements apply only to the recorded envi-
ronment and generated cases. The article does not claim a new generic matrix multiplication exponent.
Fast matrix multiplication and its stability form a separate theory [26, 10]. It also does not claim that
every structured matrix has a profitable GPU realization.
