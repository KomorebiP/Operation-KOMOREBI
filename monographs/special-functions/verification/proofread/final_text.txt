Preface
Structure beyond a finite window
The theory of special functions begins with explicit definitions, but the information those definitions
carry need not be visible in a small sample of values. A contraction in a differential equation can
determine the growth of an entire function. A prime omitted from an arithmetic sum can change its
exact parameter signature. A finite collection of positive moment matrices can conceal a negative mass
in the underlying measure.
This volume studies these distinctions through five families. Scalar nonlinear feedback leads to entire
functions with explicit growth laws. Arithmetic depth leads to positive kernels and an exact inverse
problem. Prime sieves give a setting in which positivity and finite detection can be separated. Zero or-
bits provide spectral coordinates. Coupled contraction functions bring local coefficients, global growth
and finite observation into one system.
The first seven chapters develop the constructions, their principal arguments and worked examples.
Ten appendices supply the longer proofs and computational details. The evidence register, glossary
and references follow at the back. Figures accompany the mathematics they explain, rather than form-
ing a separate gallery.
Each result is read with its own hypotheses. Classical inputs are identified where they enter. Ex-
act rational certificates, numerical illustrations and analytic proofs remain distinct, and unresolved
questions retain their stated scope. A change of representation does not, by itself, establish historical
priority.
The recurring question is precise: what can be recovered from finite information, and what additional
resolution is needed to distinguish the underlying structure?

Reading map
Five families, one mathematical question
This monograph investigates structural invariants in five families of special functions. Feedback scales,
arithmetic frequencies, signed prime masses, rotation orbits, and coupled channels provide distinct
settings. Their connections are established through specific formulas, invariants, and obstructions,
not assumed from a shared vocabulary.
The argument starts with entire functions defined by contracted nonlinear feedback. It then studies
arithmetic generating functions and prime-sieve measures, before passing to orbit spectral zeta func-
tions and exact coefficient laboratories. Coupled entire systems connect several of these ideas, while a
resolution atlas measures the difference between finite observation and global mathematical structure.
The main chapters give defining formulas, stated hypotheses, mathematical consequences, and compu-
tational figures. Extended proofs follow as technical appendices. The final notes distinguish analytic
proofs, finite checks, classical inputs, and questions that remain open.
Four starting constructions
The first four families lead to distinct results: an entire-growth law, an obstruction to finite differential
equations, a positivity boundary, and a rotation-invariant spectral coordinate. A coupled fifth family
is developed in Part IV.

Theorem statements below concern exact infinite objects under the displayed hypotheses. Plots and

The weak-tap example now has both sides quantified
For the bank with scales (1/4, 1/4) and (3/4, 3/4), weights 1 −ε, ε, the functions converge on every

Identifying a bank has unavoidable equivalences
Permuting channels, swapping the scales within a channel, or merging duplicate scale pairs by adding
their weights leaves the function unchanged. The two-scale single-channel inverse is exact up to swap-
ping. A general minimal-bank identifiability theorem beyond these equivalences is not proved.
The resulting research target is specific: determine optimal growth type, possible logarithmic oscilla-
tions, and minimal-bank identifiability. A finite plot cannot settle any of those questions.

Strict positive complex kernel
For distinct complex points si, Kij = A(si + sj) is strictly positive definite. A zero quadratic form
would force a finite exponential sum to vanish at every λn. Their accumulation at zero invokes the

D.9 Source comparison and bounded search
The continuation proof uses the finite-order Hadamard product and the beta and reflection identi-
ties. DLMF [4, §§5.5, 5.12 and 10.46] provides the special-function identities and identifies the seed
as a Mittag–Leffler function. The finite-order factorization theorem is also summarized in Elkies’s
notes [6]. The normalization, zero counts, complex branch gap and domination estimates needed here
were proved in Sections D.2–D.5.
Mellin subtraction and spectral regularization are established methods. Voros [8, §§2.1–2.2], with its
published erratum [9], provides a related framework. The present proof checks the hypotheses for
this Mittag–Leffler seed directly and does not import a theorem about Riemann zeros. The coefficient
approach also has antecedents in the hypergeometric zero spectra of Byrnes, Jiu, Moll and Vignat [7].
This comparison establishes methodological context, not priority for the particular specialization. The
archived research notes preserve the broader search record. A complete historical review of Mittag–
Leffler and hyper-Bessel zero-zeta functions remains outside the scope of this volume.

I.4 What the three examples establish together
The three resolution controls act on different mathematical objects. Radius controls what part of an
entire function is sampled. Precision controls whether two different arithmetic parameters are distin-
guishable within an observation model. Degree controls the richness of a positivity test. The common
lesson has a precise form: a property determined by an unbounded collection of tests need not have a
uniform margin on each bounded subcollection.
Here the positive tap changes a proved global order. The excluded prime changes an exactly recover-
able radical. The signed sieve lies outside the exact positivity domain even while a whole low-degree
polynomial space passes. Each conclusion follows from explicit formulas and local proofs. There is
no claim that the three mechanisms are identical, and no historical novelty claim for interpolation,
spectral peeling, or the use of isolating polynomials.

A common finite-resolution lesson
