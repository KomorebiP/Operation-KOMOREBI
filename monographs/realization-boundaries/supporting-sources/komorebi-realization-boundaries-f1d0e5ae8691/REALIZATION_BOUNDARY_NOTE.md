# Finite Realization Boundaries in a Squarefree Group Universe

*A self-contained mathematical working note for Realization Calculus*

## Scope and claim status

This note distinguishes finite operator-seed generation, finite syntactic specification, effective group presentation, and topological realization. These are genuinely different properties. The propositions below are proved mathematically in the text, using standard results (Chinese remainder theorem, the halting set's non-enumerability, and the eventual periodicity of unary automata). The assertions are not represented as historically new group-theoretic discoveries. Rather, they provide exact examples and a classification framework for studying realization boundaries. The accompanying Python program checks only finite instances. Lean compilation and independent external review have **not** been performed on this note.

**Index convention.** Let \(p_0=2,p_1=3,p_2=5,\ldots\) list distinct primes in increasing order. Write \(C_p=\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z\), additively.

## The ambient group and its finite stages

For \(N\ge1\), put

\[
A_N=\prod_{n<N}C_{p_n},\qquad m_N=\prod_{n<N}p_n.
\]

The Chinese remainder theorem gives a canonical coordinate isomorphism \(\mathbb Z/m_N\mathbb Z\simeq A_N\). Hence \(A_N\) is cyclic, generated by \(u_N=(1,\ldots,1)\).

There are two different connecting systems: the direct system \(A_N\hookrightarrow A_{N+1}\), adding a zero coordinate, and the inverse system \(A_{N+1}\twoheadrightarrow A_N\), forgetting the last coordinate. Define

\[
D=\varinjlim_N A_N=\bigoplus_{n\ge0}C_{p_n},\qquad
P=\varprojlim_N A_N=\prod_{n\ge0}C_{p_n}.
\]

The direct sum consists of finitely supported sequences; the direct product contains all sequences. Equip \(P\) with the product of the discrete topologies of its finite factors.

### Theorem 1 (direct/inverse realization separation)

1. \(A_N\) is cyclic for every \(N\).
2. As a module over its full endomorphism ring, \(D\) has seed rank \(\aleph_0\).
3. \(P\) is topologically cyclic, with \(u=(1,1,\ldots)\) a dense cyclic generator.
4. \(D\) is a proper dense subgroup of \(P\), and \(D=\operatorname{Tor}(P)\).
5. The profinite completion \(\widehat D\) is canonically \(P\).

**Proof.** (1) is the Chinese remainder theorem. For (2), any group endomorphism of \(D\) preserves the \(p_n\)-primary component: \(p_nx=0\) implies \(p_nT(x)=T(p_nx)=0\). As there is only one such factor, \(\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb Z}(D)\cong\prod_n\mathbb F_{p_n}\), acting by coordinatewise scalars. Every finite set of seeds is supported in finitely many prime coordinates, and its entire endomorphism hull remains supported there. Thus there is no finite generating seed family, while the countably many standard coordinate vectors do generate \(D\).

For (3), every finite projection of \(\langle u\rangle\) onto \(A_N\) is surjective: \(ku\) realizes any prescribed finite tuple by CRT. Such cylinder sets form a basis of the topology, so \(\overline{\langle u\rangle}=P\). For (4), finite-support vectors are dense because every basic cylinder is met by one; an element of the product has finite order exactly when its nonzero coordinates occur at finitely many distinct primes. The element \(u\) has infinite order, hence \(D\ne P\).

For (5), every homomorphism \(f:D\to F\) into a finite group vanishes on \(C_p\) whenever \(p\nmid |F|\). Hence \(f\) factors through \(A_N\) for sufficiently large \(N\). The finite projection kernels are therefore cofinal among finite-index normal subgroups, making the profinite completion the displayed inverse limit. \(\square\)

**Interpretation.** Finite seed generation is not continuous under direct limits. Completion may add a new globally generating element even when all finite-stage generators lie in the dense torsion subgroup. This is a statement about the chosen algebraic/topological realization contract, not a claim that \(D\) lacks a finite *recursive description*: it does have one.

## The packet-transfer obstruction certificate

The first theorem can be expressed in exactly the projector/transfer language of Realization Calculus. Let \(X=\bigoplus_{i\in I}X_i\) be a direct sum with packet projections \(P_i\). Assume every primitive operator \(T\) in the chosen grammar has block support in a directed graph \(E\), meaning \(P_jTP_i=0\) whenever the edge \(i\to j\) is forbidden. For a finite seed family \(S\), let \(F\) be the union of its packet supports and let \(\operatorname{Reach}_E(F)\) include vertices accessible by finite directed paths from \(F\).

### Theorem 1.1 (finite-seed reachability certificate)

For the operator algebra generated by the grammar,

\[
\operatorname{Hull}(S)\subseteq\bigoplus_{i\in\operatorname{Reach}_E(F)}X_i.
\]

If some nonzero packet \(X_j\) is not reachable, the packet projection \(P_j\) certifies non-generation:

\[
P_j(\operatorname{Hull}(S))=0\quad\text{but}\quad P_jX=X_j\ne0.
\]

**Proof.** Each primitive operation maps support only along allowed edges. Induction on the length of an operator word confines every orbit element to packets reachable by a path. Finite sums preserve that containment. If a packet \(j\) is inaccessible, its projection annihilates the entire hull but does not annihilate \(X\). \(\square\)

For \(X=D\) as an additive abelian group, take \(X_n=C_{p_n}\). Since group homomorphisms between distinct prime-order cyclic factors vanish, every group-endomorphism transfer block satisfies

\[
P_jTP_i=0\qquad(i\ne j).
\]

The transfer graph has only loops. For any finite seed support \(F\), every prime outside \(F\) supplies an explicit obstruction certificate \(P_j\). Unlike an abstract counting argument, this gives a concrete witness for each failed seed proposal. It also identifies the necessary change of grammar: to generate new prime sectors systematically, one must permit a *typed operation that changes the object* (such as adjoining a new cyclic factor) or a limit constructor, rather than merely enlarging the collection of endomorphisms of the same fixed group.

## A subgroup universe isomorphic to Cantor space

For \(B\subseteq\mathbb N\), define

\[
D_B=\bigoplus_{n\in B} C_{p_n}\ \le D,\qquad
\sigma_p(G)=1\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists x\in G\setminus\{0\}:px=0.
\]

### Theorem 2 (complete subgroup classification)

The map \(B\mapsto D_B\) is an order-isomorphism from \(\mathcal P(\mathbb N)\) onto the **entire** subgroup lattice of \(D\). Every subgroup is fully invariant under all endomorphisms of \(D\). Moreover \(D_B\cong D_C\) if and only if \(B=C\).

**Proof.** If \(x\in H\le D\) has nonzero coordinates at a finite set \(F\) of distinct primes, CRT supplies, for each \(p_n\in F\), an integer \(k_n\) with \(k_nx\) nonzero only at coordinate \(n\). That element generates the entire \(C_{p_n}\) component. Therefore \(H\) is the direct sum of the entire prime components encountered in its elements, for a unique set of indices \(B\). Endomorphisms preserve each primary component, hence preserve each \(D_B\). Finally \(n\in B\iff\sigma_{p_n}(D_B)=1\), so isomorphism determines \(B\). \(\square\)

In particular,

\[
D_B+D_C=D_{B\cup C},\qquad
D_B\cap D_C=D_{B\cap C},\qquad
D_B\oplus D_{\mathbb N\setminus B}=D.
\]

Give \(\operatorname{Sub}(D)\) the topology of pointwise subgroup-membership tests, as a subspace of \(\{0,1\}^D\). The classification is a homeomorphism \(\operatorname{Sub}(D)\simeq\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\): membership of any finite-support \(x\) depends only on finitely many selected-prime bits, while the inverse reads whether each coordinate generator belongs to the subgroup.

### Corollary 2.1 (finite-view nonidentifiability)

For every finite set \(F\subseteq\mathbb N\) and every fixed selection \(B\cap F\), there are continuum many pairwise nonisomorphic groups \(D_C\) with \(C\cap F=B\cap F\). Thus any finite number of prime-membership observations leaves continuum many possible global realizations.

For \(F_N=\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\), the compatible finite selection systems satisfy

\[
\varprojlim_N\mathcal P(F_N)\cong\mathcal P(\mathbb N).
\]

A full coherent family identifies a unique subgroup, but a finite observation set does not. Crucially, *existence of the compatible global subgroup does not imply its description by a finite grammar*.

## Exactly when is a realization computable?

A **computable group presentation** is an isomorphic copy of a group with effectively enumerable domain, computable group operations, and decidable equality. A finite or recursively enumerable list of *relators* is a different, weaker notion.

A set \(B\subseteq\mathbb N\) is **computably enumerable (c.e.)** if some algorithm lists exactly its members (without necessarily deciding nonmembership).

### Theorem 3 (prime-spectrum computability criterion)

\[
\boxed{D_B\text{ has a computable group presentation}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B\text{ is c.e.}.}
\]

**Proof, necessity.** In any computable presentation of \(D_B\), for each \(n\), enumerate elements \(x\) until finding \(x\ne0\) with \(p_nx=0\). Operations and equality make the test decidable for each candidate. A witness exists exactly when \(n\in B\). Dovetail these searches for all \(n\); they enumerate \(B\), proving it is c.e.

**Proof, sufficiency.** If \(B\) is infinite and c.e., list its distinct members effectively as \(b_0,b_1,\ldots\). Every \(b_i\) can be computed by waiting for the \((i+1)\)-st distinct enumerated member. Represent elements by canonical finite-support tuples \((a_i)\), with \(a_i\in\mathbb F_{p_{b_i}}\); addition, inversion, normalization, and equality are computable. This gives a computable group presentation. If \(B\) is finite, \(D_B\) is a finite group and trivially has a computable presentation; this last existence claim need not be uniform in an index for the c.e. enumeration. \(\square\)

The same proof **relativizes**: for every oracle \(X\), the group \(D_B\) has an \(X\)-computable presentation exactly when \(B\) is c.e. relative to \(X\).

### Construction 3.1 (an infinite group with no computable group presentation)

Let \(K=\{n:\text{program }n\text{ halts on input }n\}\). Set

\[
B=\{2n:n\in\mathbb N\}\ \cup\ \{2n+1:n\notin K\},
\qquad H=D_B.
\]

The set \(B\) is not c.e.: if it were, filtering its odd elements would enumerate \(\overline K\), contrary to the halting theorem. Theorem 3 therefore proves that \(H\) has no computable group presentation. It is still countably infinite, abelian, and locally cyclic: every finitely generated subgroup is finite cyclic.

**Boundary of this claim.** This group nevertheless has a finite *meta-mathematical definition* using \(K\). It also admits an effectively enumerated family of infinitely many generators and relators: impose commuting prime-order generators for all even and odd indexed primes, then kill the odd generator \(2n+1\) whenever program \(n\) is discovered to halt. Hence **recursive relator enumeration is not the same as computable group operations and equality**. No claim of absolute finite indescribability follows from Theorem 3.

## An independent, purely structural grammar obstruction

Define the **unary-selector grammar** \(\mathfrak G_{\mathrm{unary}}\): a finite-state automaton reads a word \(1^n\), and the associated group is the direct sum of \(C_{p_n}\) exactly for accepted indices \(n\).

### Theorem 4 (unary grammar classification)

\[
D_B\text{ is realizable by }\mathfrak G_{\mathrm{unary}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B\text{ is ultimately periodic}.
\]

**Proof.** A deterministic automaton on a unary alphabet visits a finite sequence of states, so its state sequence is eventually periodic; hence its accepting set is ultimately periodic. Conversely, a finite prefix followed by a periodic acceptance rule is implemented by a finite-state path entering a cycle. Isomorphism of two \(D_B\) groups preserves the accepted prime set by Theorem 2. \(\square\)

### Construction 4.1 (a computable group outside the unary grammar)

\[
L=\bigoplus_{k\ge0}C_{p_{2^k}}.
\]

The index set \(\{2^k:k\ge0\}\) is not ultimately periodic. If it were eventually periodic with period \(d>0\), choose \(2^k\) past the threshold with \(2^k>d\). Periodicity would require \(2^k+d\) to be another power of two, even though \(2^k<2^k+d<2^{k+1}\). Contradiction. Thus \(L\) is not unary-selector realizable. It *is* computably presentable by its explicit prime-index enumeration. An automaton reading **binary** indices can recognize powers of two, so this obstruction is specific to the unary grammar.

## Realization hierarchy

For groups within the precisely defined family \(D_B\):

| Realization contract | Exact allowed index sets \(B\) | Example outside the contract |
|---|---|---|
| Finite-state unary prime selector | Ultimately periodic | \(\{2^k\}\) |
| Total computable prime-membership selector | Decidable sets | The halting set \(K\) |
| Computable group multiplication and equality | Computably enumerable sets | \(\overline K\) |
| Arbitrary set-indexed algebraic direct sums | Every subset of \(\mathbb N\) | None within this family |

The last row is an infinitary mathematical existence contract, **not** a countable finite-code grammar. All inclusions shown are strict. A group can be realizable at a stronger level while not realizable at a weaker one.

For any grammar with countably many parameter-free finite codes and single-valued isomorphism semantics, only countably many isomorphism classes can be realized. Theorem 2 supplies continuum many candidate groups \(D_B\), so most lie outside such a grammar. That counting observation alone does not effectively identify a missing group. Theorems 3 and 4 instead provide concrete structural obstructions under stated contracts.

## Implications for Realization Calculus and Komorebi Space

The constructions reveal three separable obligations:

1. **Algebraic consistency and existence:** are the finite data compatible with a global object?
2. **Finite structural expression:** does the global object have an admissible finite construction in the chosen grammar?
3. **Effective semantics:** can the construction yield computable operations and decidable equality, or only an abstract presentation or enumerated relators?

The squarefree ambient group shows all three can diverge. Complete compatible finite views always specify a subgroup \(D_B\), but many such subgroups are not effectively presentable, and continuum many are not expressible with finite codes in any fixed countable syntax. Conversely, topology can turn the direct-limit finite-seed obstruction into a single topological generator after completion, without making the direct sum itself finite-seed generated.

The next nontrivial program is to develop reusable **intrinsic obstruction certificates** for grammars richer than prime-set selectors. Candidates include constraints derived from transfer spectra, invariants of quotient/extension functors, bounded-support properties under primitive operators, and complexity lower bounds preserved by allowed realizations.

## Reproduction, verification, and limitations

Run `python exact_checks.py`. The script uses only the Python standard library, checks CRT projections in all tuples through the first four primes, samples exact CRT reconstructions at five primes, verifies the eight subgroups of \(C_{30}\), and checks small power-of-two index differences. All these are **finite sanity checks only**. Infinite assertions are supported by the written mathematical proofs, not inferred from the script. No Lean environment was available for compilation at the time of preparation.

Potential literature overlap is substantial: finite cyclic decomposition, profinite completion, computable structures, unary-language periodicity, and subgroup classification are established mathematical areas. The added value claimed here is the precise alignment of these examples with the distinct realization contracts; historical novelty of the alignment is not established.
