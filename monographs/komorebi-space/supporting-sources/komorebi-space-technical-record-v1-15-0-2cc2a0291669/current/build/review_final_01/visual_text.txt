How to use this guide
Start with a picture and change one ingredient. Keep a boundary value fixed. Remove an observation.
Rotate a correction direction. Tighten a solver tolerance. The following investigations show what moves,
what survives, and what can fail.
Each page supplies a small declared model, a figure to inspect, and a worked change to that model. The
monograph references lead to the definitions and proofs. You can follow the pictures in sequence or
choose a question from the contents.
Three things to identify
1. The allowed objects. Integer points, real vectors, matrices, and infinite sequences have different
completion rules. A line drawn through integer markers does not create new integer solutions.
2. The information held fixed. An exact answer may exist but fail to preserve the prescribed data. Two
pictures of exactness can describe different correction problems.
3. The measured quantity. A residual, a correction norm, a movement budget, and a coordinate norm
are different numbers. Axis labels and metrics are part of the example.
Reading the graphics
Green marks the principal admissible object or successful route. Ochre and red distinguish comparisons,
discrepancies, or excluded cases. Labels and legends identify what each color and line pattern measures.
The spatial figures use genuine three-dimensional coordinates. Curves are evaluated from the formulas
stated in the examples; they are not fitted to observations.
Finite windows of infinite sequences illustrate explicit formulas. An exact tail norm or a convergence
condition comes from the accompanying mathematical argument, not from the end of a plotted line.
Numerical drawing coordinates are rounded for display.
The companions
The monograph develops the general results and their hypotheses. The Lean guide identifies exact formal
declarations and their scope. These visual investigations include concrete instances and illustrative
variations; they are not individually asserted to be Lean formalizations. The source package contains the
plotting scripts and the recorded checks for the worked values.

What survives when the order is reversed?
Apply the same two matrices to v = (1, 2).
ZXv = (2, −1)
XZv = (−2, 1) = −ZXv
X(x, y) = (y, x);  Z(x, y) = (x, −y).  The two-bit sum is the same; the sign differs.
Read the picture. Both routes use the same two-bit labels, whose sum is (1, 1). Their final vectors are
negatives because ZX = −XZ. In this model the phase c(x, y) = x2y1 records that sign. The composition
law remains associative; reversing these two factors is the operation that changes the result.
Change one thing. Attach a sign bit to the two-bit label. These two routes then end at different sign bits
over the same label (1, 1), retaining the distinction that the four labels alone omit.
