Uniform recurrences and complete mixed-linear order classification

Komorebi P. Final edition 1.0. 21 November 2026.

Abstract

We study Hankel determinants built from polynomially weighted central-binomial moments and first-order differential operators. For quadratic weights with the identity operator, we prove an explicit uniform constant-coefficient recurrence of order at most 63 and exhibit a specialization with minimal eventual order 63. For linear weights and arbitrary first-order operators, we prove a uniform bound of 112, show that it is generically attained, and give a complete finite algebraic rule for the minimal eventual order at every parameter over a characteristic-zero field. The classification includes zero parameters, repeated roots, spectral collisions, and their intersections. Exact portable certificates support the finite algebraic leaves of the proofs. The empty determinant is retained separately from recurrences beginning at index one.

This edition consolidates Theorems K, L, M, and N from the completed source edition. Its mathematical review provenance is project self-review. Independent review, historical novelty, and Lean formalization are unassessed.

1. Results, conventions, and reading guide

Let the coefficient field have characteristic zero. For the mixed linear family, fix four field elements and define

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{2m}&=u(a+bm)\binom{2m}{m},\\ \mu_{2m+1}&=v(2m+1)(a+bm)\binom{2m}{m},\\ W_N&=\det(\mu_{i+j})_{0\le i,j<N},\qquad W_0=1. \end{aligned} \tag{E1} \]

These are the moments obtained by applying the operator \(\mathcal D=u+v\partial_x\) to the even-moment functional with weight \(P(m)=a+bm\). The elementary formal binomial expansion \((1-4z)^{-1/2}=\sum_{m\ge0}\binom{2m}{m}z^m\) explains the central-binomial kernel. The proofs below work with formal Laurent series; they require no positive measure or endpoint integrability.

Write \(S\) for the forward shift: \((SW)_N=W_{N+1}\). An eventual scalar constant-coefficient recurrence of order \(r\) is an identity \(\sum_{j=0}^r d_jW_{N+j}=0\), with \(d_r=1\), for all sufficiently large \(N\). Its coefficients belong to the original field. The minimal eventual order is the least such \(r\); order zero means eventual vanishing. It is distinct from the size of a transfer matrix or the degree of an unreduced candidate denominator.

Main result. Theorems M and N together determine the minimal eventual order of (E1) for every \((a,b,u,v)\). Theorem L supplies a uniform bound of 112 and its generic sharpness. The reduced recurrences in the classification hold from \(N=1\); the convention \(W_0=1\) is separate.

To classify a mixed-linear parameter, first test whether both weight coefficients or both operator coefficients are zero; the order is then zero. For every other point on \(abuv=0\), read the ordered table in Theorem M. For \(abuv\ne0\), use \(t=b/a\) and \(q=(v/u)^2\), form the degree-four and degree-sixteen polynomials in Theorem N, and compute the stated gcd degrees and collision correction. This is a finite algebraic rule; it does not require numerical root separation or recurrence fitting.

The quadratic functional developed in section 2 provides the common proof foundation. Section 3 then treats general quartic functionals and derives (E1). Sections 4 and 5 complete its boundary and nonzero classifications, respectively. Section 6 explains the certificate logic and gives one-command reproduction instructions.

The letters K, L, M, and N, and their equation labels, are retained so the paper can be checked against the archived sources. Subsection references such as K.6 or N.3 refer within the corresponding theorem section. Auxiliary symbols are local to their sections: in particular, the polynomial coefficient \(q\) in K is distinct from the normalized ratio \(q\) in N. The normalization of \(R\) is restated whenever it changes.

The exact order explorer accepts rational normalized parameters and links to this paper. The theorem also covers algebraic parameters, including the order-64 triple-root case. Negative rational \(q\) is interpreted in a quadratic extension containing its square root; it is not a claim that a negative number is a real square.

2. The formal functional and the quadratic bound

K.0. Statement

Work over a characteristic-zero field. Put

\[ \begin{aligned} P(m)&=a+bm+cm(m-1),\\ \mu_{2m}&=P(m)\binom{2m}{m},\qquad \mu_{2m+1}=0,\\ W_N&=\det(\mu_{i+j})_{0\le i,j<N},\qquad W_0=1. \end{aligned} \]

Let \(S\) denote the forward shift in \(N\), and set

\[ \begin{aligned} A&=4a^2,\\ p&=-8a+8b-24c,\\ q&=24a^2-32ab-96ac+16b^2,\\ g(X)&=X^4+pX^3+qX^2+ApX+A^2,\\ k(X)&=X^4+4(b-2a)X^3+(24a^2-16ab+48ac)X^2 +16a^2(b-2a)X+16a^4,\\ \Gamma(X)&=(X-2a)g(X)k(X). \end{aligned} \]

Theorem K. For every \(a,b,c\), including all degree drops and the zero weight,

\[ \boxed{\Gamma(S)^7W_N=0\quad(N\ge1).} \tag{K1} \]

This is a uniform constant-coefficient recurrence of order at most 63. The previously certified specialization \((a,b,c)=(1,1,1)\), or \(P(m)=1+m^2\), has minimal eventual order 63, so the uniform bound is optimal. Minimality at every specialization is not claimed.

K.1. A fixed formal moment functional

All expansions below are formal Laurent series at infinity. Define

\[ \nu[f]=[x^{-1}]f(x)(x^2-4)^{-5/2}, \qquad (x^2-4)^{-5/2}=x^{-5}(1-4x^{-2})^{-5/2}. \]

Thus odd moments vanish, the first four moments vanish, and

\[ \nu[x^{2m}]=\frac{m(m-1)}{12}\binom{2m}{m}\quad(m\ge0). \tag{K2} \]

For \(m\ge2\), this follows by expanding the binomial series; both sides are zero at \(m=0,1\). The elementary central-binomial ratios also give

\[ \nu[(x^2-4)x^{2m}]=\frac m2\binom{2m}{m},\qquad \nu[(x^2-4)^2x^{2m}]=\binom{2m}{m}. \]

Consequently the functional defining \(\mu_j\) is exactly \(R\nu\), where

\[ R(x)=a(x^2-4)^2+2b(x^2-4)+12c. \tag{K3} \]

This identity concerns formal moments; no positive measure or endpoint integrability is assumed.

K.2. Orthogonal polynomials after the singular initial block

Write \(\sigma=x^2-4\). For \(n\ge5\), define

\[ L_n=\frac{(2n-5)!}{(n-5)!},\qquad p_n(x)=\frac{\sigma^{5/2}}{L_n} \frac{d^n}{dx^n}\sigma^{n-5/2}. \tag{K4} \]

The derivative is \(\sigma^{-5/2}\) times a polynomial of degree \(n\), whose leading coefficient is \(L_n\); hence \(p_n\) is monic. Formal integration by parts uses \([x^{-1}]f'=0\), and proves \(\nu[p_nx^j]=0\) for \(j<n\). Its squared norm is

\[ \begin{aligned} h_n=\nu[p_n^2]&=\nu[p_nx^n]\\ &=\frac{(-1)^n n!}{L_n} (-4)^{n-2}\binom{n-5/2}{n-2} =\frac{2n(n-1)}{(n-3)(n-4)}\ne0. \end{aligned}\tag{K5} \]

The size-five moment matrix of \(\nu\) is anti-triangular with anti-diagonal entries one, so its determinant is one. Changing the monomial basis to \(1,x,x^2,x^3,x^4,p_5,\ldots,p_{N-1}\) now proves

\[ H_N(\nu)=2^{N-5}\frac{(N-1)(N-2)^2(N-3)^2(N-4)}{144} \quad(N\ge5). \tag{K6} \]

In particular every required moment matrix is nonsingular, although the smaller initial matrices are singular.

The operator \(\mathcal L=\sigma\partial_x^2-3x\partial_x\) is formally self-adjoint for \(\nu\), since \((\sigma\sigma^{-5/2})'=-3x\sigma^{-5/2}\). Orthogonality and nonsingularity therefore give

\[ \mathcal Lp_n=n(n-4)p_n. \tag{K7} \]

The monic polynomial solution is unique: the difference of the eigenvalues at degrees \(n\) and \(j<n\) is \((n-j)(n+j-4)\ne0\).

Let \(x=z+z^{-1}\), \(d_n=(n-3)(n-4)\), and

\[ F_n(z)=z^n\ell_n(z),\qquad \ell_n(z)=d_n-2n(n-4)z^{-2}+n(n-1)z^{-4}. \]

Direct substitution into (K7), using \(\partial_x=(1-z^{-2})^{-1}\partial_z\), gives

\[ d_np_n(z+z^{-1})=F_n(z)+F_n(z^{-1})\quad(n\ge5). \tag{K8} \]

The right side is a symmetric Laurent polynomial, with leading term \(d_nx^n\), so uniqueness applies. The direct substitution is also retained as an exact Laurent-polynomial identity in the replay program.

K.3. The transformed determinant, without an external theorem

For generic parameters write

\[ R(x)=a(x-r)(x+r)(x-s)(x+s),\quad r=z+z^{-1},\quad s=w+w^{-1}. \]

Here \(a,r,s,rs(r^2-s^2)\) are nonzero; excluded specializations are recovered in subsection K.6. The determinant identity needed is

\[ W_N=a^NH_N(\nu) \frac{\det(p_{N+j}(t_i))_{0\le i,j<4}} {\Delta(t_0,t_1,t_2,t_3)}, \quad(t_0,t_1,t_2,t_3)=(r,-r,s,-s),\quad N\ge5. \tag{K9} \]

For completeness, let \(Q=R/a\), and let \(\nu^{\otimes N}\) act on separate polynomial variables. Expanding two determinants shows \(H_N(Q\nu)=\nu^{\otimes N}[\Delta(x)^2\prod_iQ(x_i)]/N!\). Also \(\Delta(x,t)=\Delta(x)\Delta(t)\prod_iQ(x_i)\), because there are four added roots. Express the augmented Vandermonde in any monic basis in its first \(N\) columns and \(p_N,\ldots,p_{N+3}\) in its last four columns. After expansion along the \(x\) rows and application of \(\nu^{\otimes N}\), orthogonality kills every selection containing a last-four column. The sole remaining selection gives \(N!H_N(\nu)\det(p_{N+j}(t_i))\), with positive Laplace sign. This proves (K9), using no orthogonal polynomials below degree five.

Put \(P_n(r)=F_n(z)+F_n(z^{-1})\), and similarly at \(s\). Parity gives

\[ \begin{aligned} D_N&=P_N(r)P_{N+2}(s)-P_{N+2}(r)P_N(s),\\ E_N&=P_{N+1}(r)P_{N+3}(s)-P_{N+3}(r)P_{N+1}(s). \end{aligned} \]

Row and column operations give \(\det(P_{N+j}(t_i))=4D_NE_N\), and \(\Delta(r,-r,s,-s)=4rs(s^2-r^2)^2\). Moreover

\[ \prod_{j=0}^3d_{N+j}=N(N-1)^2(N-2)^2(N-3)^2(N-4). \]

Substitution in (K9) yields

\[ W_N=\frac{(2a)^N D_NE_N}{4608N(N-1)rs(s^2-r^2)^2} \quad(N\ge5). \tag{K10} \]

K.4. Nine bases and polynomial degree six

For \(u\in\{z,z^{-1}\}\), \(v\in\{w,w^{-1}\}\), define polynomials in the formal variable \(n\):

\[ \begin{aligned} B_n(u,v)&=\ell_n(u)v^2\ell_{n+2}(v) -u^2\ell_{n+2}(u)\ell_n(v),\\ C_n(u,v)&=uv\{\ell_{n+1}(u)v^2\ell_{n+3}(v) -u^2\ell_{n+3}(u)\ell_{n+1}(v)\}. \end{aligned} \]

Each has degree at most four in \(n\). Since

\[ \ell_1(u)=6(1+u^{-2}),\qquad u^2\ell_3(u)=6(1+u^{-2}), \]

we have \(B_1=0\) and \(C_0=0\). Hence \(b_n=B_n/(n-1)\) and \(c_n=C_n/n\) are polynomials of degree at most three. These are polynomial quotients, including at indices zero and one. Expanding (K10) therefore gives the explicit exponential polynomial

\[ \mathcal W_N=\frac{(2a)^N}{4608rs(s^2-r^2)^2} \sum_{\epsilon,\eta,\delta,\theta\in\{-1,1\}} b_N(z^\epsilon,w^\eta)c_N(z^\delta,w^\theta) (z^{\epsilon+\delta}w^{\eta+\theta})^N. \tag{K11} \]

Its nine possible bases are

\[ \lambda_{ij}=2az^{2i}w^{2j},\qquad i,j\in\{-1,0,1\}, \]

and their coefficients have degree at most six in \(N\). Since \((S-\lambda)^{d+1}\) annihilates \(\lambda^N\) times a degree-\(d\) polynomial, \(\prod_{i,j}(S-\lambda_{ij})^7\) annihilates (K11).

Set \(T_1=z^2+z^{-2}=r^2-2\), \(T_2=w^2+w^{-2}=s^2-2\). From (K3),

\[ T_1+T_2=4-2b/a,\qquad T_1T_2=4-4b/a+12c/a. \]

Expanding the four corner bases gives \(g\), and the four axis bases give \(k\):

\[ \prod_{i,j\in\{-1,1\}}(X-2az^{2i}w^{2j})=g(X),\qquad \prod_{v\in\{z,w\}}(X-2av^2)(X-2av^{-2})=k(X). \]

The remaining base is \(2a\). Thus the annihilator is exactly \(\Gamma^7\).

K.5. The initial boundary

Formula (K11) also gives the actual determinants at indices one through four:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal W_1&=a,\\ \mathcal W_2&=2a(a+b),\\ \mathcal W_3&=4(a+b)(a^2+2ab+6ac-2b^2),\\ \mathcal W_4&=8(a^2+2ab+6ac-2b^2) (a^2+4ab-6b^2+24ac-12bc-36c^2). \end{aligned} \tag{K12} \]

These are finite Laurent-polynomial identities, obtained by inserting \(n=1,2,3,4\) into the explicit polynomial quotients in (K11). After setting \(a=1\), substituting \(b=(8-r^2-s^2)/2\), \(c=(r^2s^2-16+8b)/12\), and multiplying by \(4608rs(s^2-r^2)^2\), both sides are Laurent polynomials in \(z,w\). The replay checks every coefficient; homogeneity restores arbitrary \(a\). Direct size-at-most-four monomial determinants give exactly (K12). Together with (K10), this identifies (K11) with \(W_N\) for every \(N\ge1\). At zero, (K11) instead gives \(1/2\); the convention \(W_0=1\) remains separate. In particular the uniform start index cannot be lowered to zero when \(a\ne0\): the residual there is \(\Gamma(0)^7/2\ne0\).

K.6. Every specialization and sharpness

So far the proof takes place in a generic extension where the displayed denominators are nonzero. For each fixed \(N\ge1\), the residue in (K1) is a polynomial in \(a,b,c\) with integer coefficients. Substituting the generic root parameters in (K3) is injective on \(\mathbb Q[a,b,c]\): the independent variables \(r^2,s^2\) have algebraically independent sum and product, and adjoining \(z,w\) is algebraic. Since the residue vanishes in this extension, it is the zero polynomial. Specialization therefore proves (K1) over every characteristic-zero field, including \(a=0\), \(c=0\), coincident roots, singular moment matrices and the zero weight.

At \((a,b,c)=(1,1,1)\), the factors become

\[ \Gamma(X)=(X-2)(X^4-24X^3-88X^2-96X+16) (X^4-4X^3+56X^2-16X+16). \]

The exact reduced-OGF certificate described in section 6 proves minimal eventual constant-coefficient order 63 at this specialization. Thus 63 is the best possible uniform bound. Theorems L, M, and N below treat the mixed linear family.

3. The uniform mixed-linear bound

L.1. The quartic-functional theorem

Use the formal functional from the sharp quadratic proof:

\[ \nu[f]=[x^{-1}]f(x)(x^2-4)^{-5/2}. \]

For any polynomial \(R(x)=r_4x^4+r_3x^3+r_2x^2+r_1x+r_0\), let

\[ \mu_j=\nu[R(x)x^j],\qquad W_N(R)=\det(\mu_{i+j})_{0\le i,j<N},\qquad W_0(R)=1. \]

Define \(C_0(x)=2\), \(C_1(x)=x\), \(C_k(x)=xC_{k-1}(x)-C_{k-2}(x)\). For \(1\le k\le16\), set

\[ T_k=2^k\operatorname{Res}_x(R,C_k). \]

Here the resultant uses the fixed degree-four coefficient list for \(R\), including when \(r_4=0\); it is the polynomial determinant of its Sylvester matrix. Define

\[ \begin{aligned} d_0&=1,\\ d_j&=-\frac1j\sum_{k=1}^j d_{j-k}T_k\quad(1\le j\le16),\\ \chi_R(X)&=\sum_{j=0}^{16}d_jX^{16-j}. \end{aligned} \tag{L1} \]

These formulas are explicit and involve only finitely many polynomial determinants and divisions by the nonzero integers 1 through 16. In particular every \(d_j\) is a polynomial over \(\mathbb Q\) in the five coefficients of \(R\); no leading-coefficient division is required.

Theorem L. Over every characteristic-zero field, for every quartic coefficient list, including degree drops and the zero polynomial,

\[ \boxed{\chi_R(S)^7W_N(R)=0\quad(N\ge1).} \tag{L2} \]

Thus these Hankel determinants have a uniform constant-coefficient recurrence of order at most 112.

L.2. Sixteen bases and degree six

The sharp quadratic proof already establishes the formal orthogonal polynomials, their nonzero norms from degree five onward, and the augmented- Vandermonde identity. Its determinant proof does not require an even \(R\). For generic \(R=r_4\prod_{i=1}^4(x-t_i)\), put \(t_i=z_i+z_i^{-1}\), and use its definitions

\[ P_n(t_i)=F_n(z_i)+F_n(z_i^{-1}),\qquad F_n(z)=z^n\ell_n(z),\qquad \ell_n(z)=(n-3)(n-4)-2n(n-4)z^{-2}+n(n-1)z^{-4}. \]

The same normalization gives, for \(N\ge5\),

\[ W_N(R)=\frac{(2r_4)^N}{4608N(N-1)\Delta(t)} \det(P_{N+j}(t_i))_{1\le i\le4,\ 0\le j<4}. \tag{L3} \]

Expand this determinant by choosing \(u_i=z_i\) or \(z_i^{-1}\) in each row. After extracting \(\prod_i u_i^N\), the coefficient determinant is

\[ Q_n(u)=\det(u_i^j\ell_{n+j}(u_i))_{1\le i\le4,\ 0\le j<4}. \]

It has degree at most eight in \(n\). At \(n=0\), columns one and three coincide, since \(u\ell_1(u)=u^3\ell_3(u)\). At \(n=1\), columns zero and two coincide, since \(\ell_1(u)=u^2\ell_3(u)\). Therefore \(Q_n/[n(n-1)]\) is a polynomial of degree at most six. Equation (L3) is an exponential polynomial with sixteen possible bases

\[ \lambda_\epsilon=2r_4\prod_{i=1}^4z_i^{\epsilon_i}, \qquad\epsilon_i\in\{-1,1\}, \tag{L4} \]

whose coefficients have degree at most six in \(N\).

Their power sums are

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_\epsilon\lambda_\epsilon^k &=(2r_4)^k\prod_i(z_i^k+z_i^{-k})\\ &=(2r_4)^k\prod_i C_k(t_i) =2^k\operatorname{Res}_x(R,C_k)=T_k. \end{aligned} \]

Newton's identities therefore identify \(\prod_\epsilon(X-\lambda_\epsilon)\) with the explicit polynomial \(\chi_R\) in (L1). To see the coefficient recursion directly, expand \(\prod_\epsilon(1-\lambda_\epsilon z)\), differentiate its formal logarithm, and compare coefficients. Each degree-six exponential component is killed by the seventh power of its base factor. This proves (L2) for the generic tail.

L.3. The boundary and every specialization

The exponential polynomial extends to index one. The necessary boundary calculation is finite and uniform in the four coefficients of a monic quartic. For \(N=2,3,4\), use

\[ P_2=4x^2+8,\qquad P_3=12x,\qquad P_4=24, \]

and \(P_n=(n-3)(n-4)p_n\) at \(n=5,6,7\). Reduce these polynomials modulo \(R=x^4+r_3x^3+r_2x^2+r_1x+r_0\). If \(M_N\) is the four-by-four matrix whose columns are the coefficient vectors of the remainders of \(P_N,\ldots,P_{N+3}\), the identities are

\[ 2^N\det M_N=4608N(N-1) \det(\nu[Rx^{i+j}])_{0\le i,j<N}. \tag{L5} \]

All coefficients of these three polynomial identities in \(\mathbb Q[r_0,r_1,r_2,r_3]\) are checked by both the exploratory symbolic calculation and the separate sparse-polynomial replay. The remainder construction is valid because the evaluation matrix is the Vandermonde matrix times \(M_N\); its determinant divided by \(\Delta(t)\) is \(\det M_N\). Homogeneity restores arbitrary nonzero \(r_4\).

For the boundary limit at one, subtract column zero from column two before dividing by \(n-1\). The vanishing-column derivative has no logarithm of \(z_i\), since \(F_3(z_i)-F_1(z_i)=0\) already branch by branch. Its sum of the two branches is

\[ J(x)=-2x^3+8x. \]

At \(N=1\), the four columns are therefore \(12x,4x^2+8,J(x),24\). Their coefficient determinant is 2304, giving \(2r_4\cdot2304/4608=r_4=\mu_0\). At zero, subtract column one from column three; the columns become \(24,12x,4x^2+8,J(x)\), with determinant -2304. The additional factor \(n-1=-1\) gives the exponential continuation value \(1/2\), whereas \(W_0=1\).

Thus the exponential formula agrees with the actual sequence for every \(N\ge1\). For each such fixed \(N\), the residue in (L2) is a polynomial over \(\mathbb Q\) in the five coefficients of \(R\). The generic proof therefore specializes to every coefficient list, including repeated roots, degree drops, singular minors and zero weights. The uniform start cannot be lowered to zero when \(r_4\ne0\): its residue there is \(\chi_R(0)^7/2=(2r_4)^{112}/2\ne0\).

L.4. Every linear weight and first-order operator

Let \(F_P[x^{2m}]=(a+bm)\binom{2m}{m}\), with odd moments zero, and let \(\mathcal D=u+v\partial_x\). The induced moments are

\[ \mu_{2m}=u(a+bm)\binom{2m}{m},\qquad \mu_{2m+1}=v(2m+1)(a+bm)\binom{2m}{m}. \tag{L6} \]

Writing \(\sigma=x^2-4\), the functional \(F_P\) is \((a\sigma^2+2b\sigma)\nu\). Formal integration by parts for the derivative term gives exactly \(R\nu\), with

\[ \begin{aligned} R(x)&=u[a\sigma^2+2b\sigma]+vx[a\sigma+6b],\\ r_4&=ua,\qquad r_3=va,\\ r_2&=u(-8a+2b),\\ r_1&=v(-4a+6b),\\ r_0&=u(16a-8b). \end{aligned} \tag{L7} \]

Indeed \(\nu[Qf']=-\nu[(Q'-5xQ/\sigma)f]\), and \(-Q'+5xQ/\sigma=x(a\sigma+6b)\) for \(Q=a\sigma^2+2b\sigma\). The quotient here is polynomial because \(\sigma\) divides \(Q\). Theorem L applied to (L7) proves the uniform order-at-most-112 recurrence for all \(a,b,u,v\), including pure odd, identity, zero and degree-drop cases.

At \((a,b,u,v)=(1,1,1,2)\), the quartic is \(x^4+2x^3-6x^2+4x+8\). Formula (L1) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \chi_R(X)={}&X^{16}-16X^{15}+384X^{14}+1600X^{13}-21568X^{12}\\ &+129792X^{11}+649216X^{10}-2378752X^9+9340416X^8\\ &-9515008X^7+10387456X^6+8306688X^5-5521408X^4\\ &+1638400X^3+1572864X^2-262144X+65536. \end{aligned} \]

This is exactly the reversed degree-16 generating-function factor in the published order-112 example. Its reduced numerator is reconstructed from the new formula and actual determinant values; the retained degree-preserving modular Bezout witness checks coprimality. Thus the uniform bound is optimal.

L.5. Generic minimality

The mixed-family minimal eventual order is 112 on a nonempty Zariski-open set of parameters. Here is the precise argument. Put

\[ D(z)=z^{112}\chi_R(z^{-1})^7,\qquad N(z)=\left[D(z)\sum_{j\ge0}W_jz^j\right]_{\le112}. \]

The recurrence proves that the generating function is \(N/D\). Both polynomial coefficient lists depend polynomially on \(a,b,u,v\). At the certified sample they have degree 112 and are coprime, so their fixed-degree resultant is a polynomial in the parameters that is nonzero at that sample. On the nonempty open set where that resultant and the leading coefficient of \(D\) are nonzero, the reduced denominator has degree 112. Every eventual constant-coefficient scalar recurrence must annihilate that denominator, so its order is at least 112. The bound proves equality there.

Theorems M and N below classify minimal orders on the exceptional parameter locus. The bound 63 for quadratic weights with the identity operator remains the separate Theorem K. The broader scope is stated in section 7.

4. The coordinate boundary

M.2. Smaller uniform recurrences from formal moment functionals

Put \(\sigma=x^2-4\), and define formal residues at infinity by

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho_1[f]&=[x^{-1}]f(x)\sigma^{-3/2},\\ \rho_2[f]&=[x^{-1}]f(x)\sigma^{-5/2}. \end{aligned} \]

The second functional is the \(\nu\) of the preceding proofs, and \(\rho_1=\sigma\rho_2\). Their even moments are respectively \(m\binom{2m}{m}/2\) and \(m(m-1)\binom{2m}{m}/12\); odd moments vanish.

For \(n\ge3\), the monic formal orthogonal polynomials for \(\rho_1\) are

\[ q_n=\frac{\sigma^{3/2}}{(2n-3)!/(n-3)!} \partial_x^n\sigma^{n-3/2}. \]

The Rodrigues expression is monic, and formal integration by parts gives orthogonality. More explicitly, its norm is

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho_1[q_n^2]&=\rho_1[q_nx^n]\\ &=\frac{(-1)^n n!}{(2n-3)!/(n-3)!}\\ &\quad{}\cdot(-4)^{n-1}\binom{n-3/2}{n-1}\\ &=-\frac{2n}{n-2}\ne0. \end{aligned} \]

The size-three base Gram determinant is -1. Consequently

\[ H_N(\rho_1)=\frac{(-2)^N(N-1)(N-2)}{16}\quad(N\ge3). \]

The operator \(\sigma\partial_x^2-x\partial_x\) is formally self-adjoint because \((\sigma\sigma^{-3/2})'=-x\sigma^{-3/2}\). Orthogonality and nonsingularity give \(\sigma q_n''-xq_n'=n(n-2)q_n\). Its monic solution is unique, since the eigenvalue difference at degree \(j<n\) is \((n-j)(n+j-2)\ne0\). With \(x=z+z^{-1}\), substitution therefore gives

\[ (n-2)q_n(x)=(n-2)C_n(x)-nC_{n-2}(x), \qquad C_n(z+z^{-1})=z^n+z^{-n}. \]

The coefficients of the two exponential branches have degree one in \(n\). These assertions also have separate exact polynomial, orthogonality and norm checks in the replay. The Rodrigues/orthogonality argument is the same formal residue argument as in Theorem K, with the displayed exponent changed; it uses no positive measure.

For a quadratic \(R=A x^2+B x+C\) multiplying \(\rho_1\), the two-root augmented-Vandermonde identity therefore has four bases and coefficient degree at most two. Their base polynomial is

\[ X^4+2CX^3+(4B^2-8AC-8A^2)X^2+8A^2CX+16A^4. \]

Indeed, if the two roots are \(r=z+z^{-1}\), \(s=w+w^{-1}\), the bases are \(-2Az^{\pm1}w^{\pm1}\). Their elementary symmetric functions use \(r+s=-B/A\) and \(rs=C/A\), giving the displayed polynomial. Its cube is a uniform order-12 annihilator. The normalization of the raw two-root determinant is \((-2A)^N/16\). Its initial indices one and two agree with the actual determinants by coefficient identities; its value at zero is 1/2. For even \(R\), the four branches give three distinct bases generically, so the bound improves to nine. The resulting characteristic factor for \(u=1\) is the previously published \((X-2a)(X^2-4(a-b)X+4a^2)\); scaling the functional by \(u\) scales every exponential base by \(u\).

For a quadratic multiplying \(\rho_2\), use the raw polynomials \(P_n=(n-3)(n-4)p_n\) from Theorem K. The normalization is

\[ \frac{(2A)^N(N-1)(N-2)}{4608}. \]

The determinant of two raw branches has coefficient degree at most four; the extra factor has degree two. Thus four bases give the uniform bound 28, with seventh powers of

\[ X^4-2CX^3+(4B^2-8AC-8A^2)X^2-8A^2CX+16A^4. \]

Indices one through four are exact coefficient identities, using the same low raw polynomials as Theorem L; the zero-index continuation is 1/8.

Finally, for the odd cubic \(R=d x(x^2-r^2)\) multiplying \(\rho_2\), the three-root determinant has normalization \((-2d)^N/4608\). Every odd-size determinant vanishes. The roots \(0,r,-r\), with \(r=z+z^{-1}\), give the six possible full-sequence bases

\[ \{\pm2id,\ \pm2id z^2,\ \pm2id z^{-2}\}. \]

Each coefficient has degree at most six, so their seventh powers give a uniform order-42 recurrence. Here \(i^2=-1\) is used in an algebraic extension only; the resulting characteristic polynomial has coefficients in the original field. For \(d=av\), \(r^2=4-6b/a\), it is

\[ \left[(X^2+4a^2v^2) \{X^4+(8a^2-96ab+144b^2)v^2X^2+16a^4v^4\}\right]^7. \]

For the initial boundary, divide the raw polynomials by the monic cubic \(x^3-r^2x\) and take the three-by-three remainder coefficient determinant. The identities at indices one through four agree coefficientwise with the actual moment determinants. At zero the continuation is 1/4.

For all three constructions, the generic calculation takes place where the displayed root determinants are nonsingular. For each fixed recurrence index, the residue is a polynomial in the original coefficients. Thus the identities extend to all specializations, including repeated roots and degree drops. No inverse singular minor or limiting inference from numeric roots is used.

N.2. The sixteen bases and their leading coefficients

Let the roots of \(R\), counted with multiplicity, be \(x_i\), and choose \(z_i\) with \(x_i=z_i+z_i^{-1}\) in an algebraic extension. For distinct roots, Theorem L gives the exponential polynomial

\[ \widehat W_N=\frac{2^N}{4608N(N-1)\Delta(x)} \det(P_{N+j}(x_i))_{1\le i\le4,\ 0\le j<4}, \tag{N4} \]

where \(P_n(x_i)=z_i^n\ell_n(z_i)+z_i^{-n}\ell_n(z_i^{-1})\) and

\[ \ell_n(z)=(n-3)(n-4)-2n(n-4)z^{-2}+n(n-1)z^{-4}. \]

Its sixteen labeled normalized bases are \(\prod_i z_i^{\epsilon_i}\), \(\epsilon_i\in\{-1,1\}\); the actual bases are \(2au\) times these. Their power sums are \(T_k\), and their multiset is closed under inversion. Thus their monic characteristic polynomial is exactly \(F\) in (N2). Replacing \(w\) by \(-w\) negates all four roots and leaves these power sums unchanged. This proves the asserted polynomial dependence on \(t,q\).

For a rowwise choice \(v_i\in\{z_i,z_i^{-1}\}\), define

\[ Q_n(v)=\det(v_i^j\ell_{n+j}(v_i))_{1\le i\le4,\ 0\le j<4}. \]

It is divisible by \(n(n-1)\), as in Theorem L. Every coefficient in \(n\) is alternating in the \(v_i\), so it is also divisible by \(\Delta(v)\) in the Laurent polynomial ring. These two factors are coprime. Consequently

\[ L_n(v)=Q_n(v)/(n(n-1)\Delta(v)) \]

is a Laurent polynomial whose degree in \(n\) is at most six. This fact controls the lower coefficients as well as the leading one; it excludes additional poles when roots merge.

Since \(x_j-x_i=(v_j-v_i)(1-(v_iv_j)^{-1})\), the coefficient of this branch has leading term \(A(v)n^6/4608\), where

\[ A(v)=\frac{\prod_i(1-v_i^{-2})^2} {\prod_{i<j}(1-(v_iv_j)^{-1})}. \tag{N5} \]

For distinct nonendpoint roots it is nonzero. The next coefficient, divided by the leading one, is

\[ -15-6S(v),\qquad S(v)=\sum_i\frac1{v_i^2-1}. \tag{N6} \]

To check (N6), use the expansion

\[ \ell_{n+j}(v)=A_0(v)n^2+(2jA_0(v)+B_0(v))n+\cdots \]

with the following coefficients:

\[ A_0(v)=(1-v^{-2})^2,\qquad B_0(v)/A_0(v)=-7-6/(v^2-1). \]

N.3. All collisions with simple roots

The nonzero hypotheses give

\[ R(2)=12tw,\qquad R(-2)=-12tw. \tag{N7} \]

No two distinct roots of \(R\) can be opposites. Indeed, if \(r,-r\) were roots with \(r\ne0\), the even and odd parts of (N1), with \(\sigma=r^2-4\), would give \(\sigma(\sigma+2t)=0\) and \(\sigma+6t=0\). Their combination is \(24t^2=0\), a contradiction. A zero root occurs only at \(t=2\); its derivative is \(8w\ne0\), so it is always simple.

Two sign vertices at Hamming distance one can collide only if some \(z_i^2=1\), contradicting (N7). At distance two, the condition is \((z_i^{\epsilon_i}z_j^{\epsilon_j})^2=1\), which would give equal or opposite roots. Thus a collision has distance three or four. There cannot be a cluster of three vertices: relative to one vertex, two distinct flip sets of sizes at least three have symmetric difference of size at most two. Every cluster therefore has size at most two.

For a distance-three pair put \(v_1v_2v_3=e\), \(e\in\{1,-1\}\). Inverting these three branches changes the ratio of (N5) by

\[ \frac{A(v_1^{-1},v_2^{-1},v_3^{-1},v_4)}{A(v)} =-\frac{\prod_{i=1}^3(v_4v_i-1)}{e\prod_{i=1}^3(v_4-v_i)}. \]

If the two leading coefficients canceled, subtraction of these cubic products would give

\[ e\prod_{i=1}^3(v_i-e)\,v_4(v_4+e)=0. \]

Every factor is nonzero: a zero factor would be a zero branch or an endpoint root. Thus the aggregate coefficient still has degree six.

For distance four, put \(V=\prod_i v_i\). Directly from (N5), \(A(v^{-1})=VA(v)\). At a positive full collision, \(V=1\), the leading terms add. At a negative full collision, \(V=-1\), they cancel. Since \(S(v^{-1})=-4-S(v)\), (N6) gives the retained degree-five coefficient

\[ -12A(v)(2+S(v))/4608. \tag{N8} \]

It is nonzero in this mixed family. Here are the necessary details. Let \(s_j\) be the elementary symmetric functions of the four \(v_i\) with \(s_4=-1\), and let \(J(z)=\prod_i(z-v_i)\). Then

\[ 2+S(v)=\frac{s_1^2-s_3^2-4s_2}{J(1)J(-1)}. \]

The root identities from (N1) give

\[ \begin{aligned} s_1-s_3&=-w,\\ s_1s_3&=4-2t,\\ s_2(s_1+s_3)&=6tw. \end{aligned} \]

If the numerator vanished, these equations would force \(q+16t+16=0\). The coefficientwise identity

\[ B(t,-16(t+1))=-576t^3 \]

rules this out at a negative full collision, because

\[ F(1)=q^2(q+36t^2)^2,\qquad F(-1)=B(t,q)^2. \tag{N9} \]

Both identities in (N9) are checked over \(\mathbb Q[t,q]\). There is exactly one cluster at the negative base when it occurs. Every other distinct base contributes order seven, and that one contributes six. This proves \(7h-\delta\) for simple roots, including every intersection of their collision conditions.

N.4. What confluence does to a coefficient degree

Suppose a root of multiplicity \(m\) is split formally as \(r+e_1,\ldots,r+e_m\), and choose a local branch \(z\), with \(r=z+z^{-1}\). Its logarithmic derivative is \(\alpha=1/(z-z^{-1})\ne0\). Choose \(k\) of the plus branches and \(m-k\) inverse branches. In the denominator following (N5), exactly the \(k(m-k)\) cross-branch factors vanish. Each has linear part \(\alpha(e_i-e_j)\), with \(i\) in the plus set and \(j\) outside it. All other factors have nonzero limits.

Group the choices of the plus set before taking the confluent divided difference. This grouped numerator is alternating in the root variables, so division by their Vandermonde is regular. Every coefficient of \(L_n\) is Laurent polynomial, and hence has no further poles. Consequently the coefficient degree can increase by at most \(d=k(m-k)\).

The leading coefficient increases by exactly \(d\). To see that it does not cancel, take the leading degree-six term and expand its exponential factor. Up to a common nonzero factor, the required local limit is

\[ \sum_{\substack{I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\\ \#I=k}} \frac{\exp(\tau\sum_{i\in I}e_i)} {\prod_{i\in I,\ j\notin I}(e_i-e_j)}, \qquad \tau=2\alpha n. \]

For the multiplicities needed here, its value at all \(e_i=0\) is

\((m,k)\)Local value

\((3,1)\)\(\tau^2/2\)
\((3,2)\)\(\tau^2/2\)

These identities are algebraic divided differences. The first is \((e^{\tau e_1}-e^{\tau e_2})/(e_1-e_2)\). The second is its second divided difference, whose coincident value is the quadratic Taylor coefficient \(\tau^2/2\). For the third, use the complement of the two-element set; the expression becomes \(e^{\tau\sum e_i}\) times the second divided difference of \(e^{-\tau x}\), with the same value. This uses only finite Taylor coefficients over a characteristic-zero field. It does not use an analytic limit or an assumption about positive measures.

All common factors are nonzero, including \(\alpha\) and the nonvanishing factors of (N5). Lower powers of \(n\) have the same pole bound and cannot cancel this highest power. Thus the exact degree of this grouped coefficient is

\[ 6+k(m-k). \]

For several repeated roots the contributions add; in particular two double roots give degree \(6+k_1(2-k_1)+k_2(2-k_2)\).

After this confluence, the coefficient functions are regular whenever the distinct root groups stay distinct and avoid the endpoints. Further spectral collisions therefore combine finite coefficient polynomials; they do not create another degree increase.

N.5. A double root and every collision on its locus

Let the roots be \(r,r,s,t_0\). The double root is nonzero, by (N7) and the zero-root derivative calculation. There are eight modes in which its two branches have the same sign; call these the A modes. They have degree six and their leading coefficients are the regular repeated-entry values of (N5). The four mixed-sign modes from that double root, the B modes, have bases \(2au\,z_s^{\pm1}z_{t_0}^{\pm1}\) and degree seven.

The four B bases are distinct and none is \(\pm2au\): otherwise the two simple roots would be equal or opposite, or an endpoint would occur. A collision involving B and one or more A modes retains degree seven, because only one B mode belongs to each such cluster.

An A-only cluster has at most two modes. Regard an A sign vector as a four-bit vector with its first two bits equal. A distance-two collision that flips those first two bits would require \(z_r^4=1\), hence \(r\in\{0,2,-2\}\), all excluded. The other distance-one and distance-two collisions were already excluded in subsection N.3. The same binary-code argument therefore applies. Distance-three pairs retain degree six; the calculation in subsection N.3 permits repeated entries in (N5). A positive full pair also retains degree six. The negative full pair has exact degree five by (N8); the symmetric identities proving its nonvanishing also permit repeated entries.

Exactly four of the \(h\) clusters contain B modes. Their contributions are eight each, and the others contribute seven, except for the one possible negative full pair. Thus the order is

\[ 4\cdot8+(h-4)\cdot7-\delta=7h+4-\delta. \]

This includes intersections of confluence with every spectral collision, without assuming that the discriminant locus is smooth.

For two double roots, write \(R=(x^2+sx+p)^2\). Coefficient comparison gives

\[ 2s=w,\quad p=6t-4,\quad s^2+2p=2t-8,\quad p^2=16-8t. \]

Since \(t\ne0\), these imply

\[ t=10/9,\qquad q=-400/9,\qquad R=(x^2+wx/2+8/3)^2. \tag{N10} \]

At this exact rational \((t,q)\), the coefficientwise characteristic replay and exact polynomial gcd give \(h=9\). There are at most nine confluent bases, indexed by \((k_1,k_2)\in\{0,1,2\}^2\), so this check proves that all nine are distinct. Four have degree six, four have degree seven, and the central mode has degree eight. Their order is \(4\cdot7+4\cdot8+9=69\). The check also gives \(\delta=0\). This is exhaustive for this root type because (N10) is forced, not inferred from a sample.

For a triple root, write \(R=(x-r)^3(x-s)\), with \(y=r^2\). Matching the two lower coefficients, after using the upper coefficients to eliminate \(t,w\), gives

\[ \begin{aligned} 26r^3+33r^2s+9rs^2+60r+20s&=0,\\ r^3s+12r^2+12rs+16&=0. \end{aligned} \]

Here \(r\ne0\), and \(y+12\ne0\), since the second equation would otherwise be \(-128=0\). Therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} s&=-\frac{4(3y+4)}{r(y+12)},\\ P(y)&=13y^4+144y^3+480y^2+1280y-768=0,\\ t&=\frac{3y^2+8y+48}{2(y+12)},\\ q&=\frac{(3y^2+24y-16)^2}{y(y+12)^2}. \end{aligned} \tag{N11} \]

Substitution into the first equation, multiplied by \(r(y+12)^2\), gives exactly \(2P(y)\); the replay checks every coefficient of this identity.

The eight potential bases are \(2au\,z_r^{2k-3}z_s^{\epsilon}\), \(k=0,1,2,3\), \(\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}\). Within a fixed sign of \(\epsilon\), a collision would require \(y\in\{0,1,4\}\). Between the two signs, it would require \(s=\pm C_j(r)\), \(j=0,1,2,3\). Put

\[ D(y)=y(y+12)^2,\qquad M(y)=16(3y+4)^2, \qquad s^2=M(y)/D(y). \]

The certificate supplies exact Bezout identities between \(P\) and each of

\[ \begin{aligned} &y(y-1)(y-4),\quad M-yD,\quad M-4D,\\ &M-(y-2)^2D,\quad M-y(y-3)^2D,\\ &y(y+12),\quad 3y^2+8y+48,\quad 3y^2+24y-16. \end{aligned} \tag{N12} \]

Each identity has the form \(UP+VQ=1\) over \(\mathbb Q[y]\), checked coefficientwise by the separate replay. The first five exclude every listed collision, and the remaining ones verify the denominators and nonzero parameters in (N11). All eight bases are therefore distinct, over every characteristic-zero field. Four coefficients have degree six and four have degree eight, giving order \(4\cdot7+4\cdot9=64\). Their distinctness also gives \(h=8\) and \(\delta=0\).

Finally, a quadruple root \(R=(x-r)^4\) would have \(r\ne0\) and, with \(y=r^2\), would require

\[ 17y+20=0,\qquad y^2+24y+16=0. \]

The latter's remainder modulo the former is \(-3136/289\ne0\). Thus no further multiplicity type exists in this nonzero family.

N.7. Minimality, coefficient field, and initial index

An exponential polynomial \(\lambda^N p(N)\), with \(\lambda\ne0\) and \(\deg p=d\), has a generating-function pole of exact order \(d+1\) at \(z=\lambda^{-1}\). Its leading pole coefficient contains the nonzero factor \(d!\) times the leading coefficient of \(p\). Distinct bases give distinct poles. Hence the minimal eventual recurrence order is exactly the sum of these \(d+1\), after combining equal bases. This proves the lower bounds as well as the annihilators counted above.

The calculations can be made in a finite splitting field. Permuting roots or inverting the chosen lifts permutes the summands. Consequently the monic minimal annihilator is invariant under the splitting field's Galois group, so it lies in the original field. Its order is unchanged by that extension. Likewise, the gcd degrees in (N3) are unchanged by field extension.

For repeated roots, (N4) uses the rows of derivatives divided by their factorials and the confluent Vandermonde \(\prod_{i<j}(r_j-r_i)^{m_im_j}\). The low-index boundary identities of Theorem L are polynomial identities in the quartic coefficients, so they remain valid under this confluence. Equivalently, the initial coefficient determinants are unchanged when evaluations are replaced by these normalized jets. Thus the exponential polynomial gives the actual \(W_N\) for every \(N\ge1\). Its continuation at zero is still \(1/2\), by the same degree-three boundary determinant as in Theorem L.

Every base is nonzero. The minimal annihilator therefore has nonzero constant coefficient, and its residual at zero is half that coefficient, since the actual empty determinant is one. The recurrence cannot be extended to index zero without increasing its order. This completes the stated classification.

6. Certificates and reproduction

6.1. How a finite certificate proves an all-index assertion

The written proofs supply the uniform annihilators and their start indices. Finite determinant values alone would not supply those conclusions. For clarity, the algebraic bridge used by the generating-function certificates is recorded here.

Certificate lemma. Suppose a sequence satisfies a monic recurrence \(A(S)W_N=0\) of order \(r\) for every \(N\ge1\). Set

\[ \begin{aligned} G(z)&=\sum_{N\ge0}W_Nz^N,\\ D(z)&=z^rA(z^{-1}),\\ H(z)&=[D(z)G(z)]_{\le r}. \end{aligned} \tag{E2} \]

Then \(D(0)=1\) and \(G=H/D\). The numerator \(H\) is determined by \(W_0,\ldots,W_r\). If proposed polynomials \(U,V\), with \(V(0)=1\), satisfy \(UD=HV\), then \(G=U/V\). If they are coprime, its reduced denominator has degree \(\deg V\), which is exactly the minimal eventual scalar constant-coefficient recurrence order.

Proof. The coefficient of \(z^{N+r}\) in \(DG\) is the stated recurrence residual. Thus every coefficient above degree \(r\) vanishes, which proves (E2) and the cross-multiplication assertion. If a monic recurrence of order \(d\) holds eventually, multiplication of \(G\) by its reversed polynomial yields a polynomial. Coprimality of \(U,V\) forces \(V\) to divide that reversed polynomial, so \(d\ge\deg V\). Conversely, multiplication by \(V\) gives an eventual recurrence of order \(\deg V\). This proves equality, including order zero.

The modular witnesses in the source package establish coprimality after clearing rational denominators. They retain both polynomial degrees and verify a Bezout identity equal to one modulo the stated prime. A nonconstant common factor over the rationals would retain a nonconstant common factor under that degree-preserving reduction, contradicting the identity. This supplies an exact lower bound, rather than an inference from a fitted list.
