## 1. Exact source and the externally supplied finiteness input

For a finite set S of rational primes let

\[
T_S=\{(a,b,c)\in\mathbf Z_{>0}^3:
a+b=c,\ \gcd(a,b)=1,\ \operatorname{supp}(abc)\subseteq S\}.
\]

Triples are ordered. Let U_S be the positive rational multiplicative group
generated by S. Its rank is s=|S|, and U_S squared has rank 2s.

Theorem 1.1 of Evertse, Schlickewei and Schmidt, in the primary author
preprint identified in SOURCE_RECORD.md, bounds the number of nondegenerate
solutions to a fixed n-variable linear equation in a rank-r multiplicative
subgroup by exp((6n)^(3n)(r+1)). We import that theorem, rather than claim to
reprove its Diophantine approximation argument.

**Proposition 1.** The map

\[
(a,b,c)\longmapsto (a/c,b/c)
\]

is a bijection from T_S to the positive solutions of x+y=1 in U_S squared.
Consequently

\[
|T_S|\leq \exp(12^6(2s+1)),
\]

and T_S is finite.

**Proof.** The image belongs to U_S squared and has sum one. Given a
positive rational solution, choose positive integers a,b,c with common
denominator c and divide all three by their common gcd. Since a+b=c,
this normalization has gcd(a,b)=1 and is unique. For any prime outside S,
the equations v_p(a)-v_p(c)=v_p(b)-v_p(c)=0 give equal valuations on all
three entries. Primitivity forces that common valuation to be zero.
Thus the normalized triple lies in T_S. The forward map is injective by
the uniqueness of primitive integer normalization. Apply the cited theorem
inside the algebraic closure of Q, with n=2, coefficients both one and
Gamma=U_S squared. The rank is 2s by unique prime factorization. A proper
nonempty subsum is x or y, each nonzero, so every solution is
nondegenerate. This proves the count. \(\square\)

The theorem supplies a number of solutions, not their maximum height.
No effective upper bound on c is extracted from that count in this route.

## 2. Consequence for the intended-source view system

Define

\[
B(S)=\max\bigl(\{1\}\cup\{c:(a,b,c)\in T_S\}\bigr).
\]

Proposition 1 proves that this integer exists for every finite S. Existence
is the assertion used here. The displayed definition is not an algorithm
to discover the final solution in an unbounded enumeration.

For B at least 2 and N at least 3, let F_N(B,S) consist of residue triples
modulo N! admitting lifts A,B',C between 1 and B, each supported on S,
such that A+B' is congruent to C modulo N! and gcd(A,B',N!)=1.
Reduction gives maps F_(N+1)(B,S) to F_N(B,S), and hence the same
contravariant Set-valued chain used in RR7, now with the support observer
retained on its bounded lift domain.

**Proposition 2.** The coherent sections of this chain are exactly the
triples in T_S with c at most B. If N is at least B+1, every element of
F_N(B,S) already has a unique ordinary primitive additive lift in that
budget. Thus, with B=max(2,B(S)), this inverse limit reconstructs all of
T_S.

**Proof.** Once N! exceeds B, each residue coordinate has at most one
lift between 1 and B. Along a coherent section those unique lifts agree
at all sufficiently high levels. Their fixed additive defect is divisible
by every sufficiently large factorial, so is zero. A common prime divisor
of A and B' would divide some sufficiently large factorial, contradicting
the primitive residue observer. Their support membership persists because
the lifts themselves stabilize. Conversely each required ordinary triple
gives a coherent residue family. For the stated finite level, N! exceeds
2B, so the additive congruence forces equality, and every possible common
prime divisor is at most B and divides N!. Unique bounded lifts then give
the assertion. \(\square\)

This is a positive source reconstruction consequence of genuine integer
arithmetic. It is not a claim that arbitrary unbounded locally supported
residue data lie in the intended source. It also does not make the unknown
budget B(S) computable from the cardinality bound.

## 3. An entirely elementary, explicit sector

**Theorem 3.** For S={2,3}, the elements of T_S are, up to interchanging
a and b,

\[
(1,1,2),\qquad (1,2,3),\qquad (1,3,4),\qquad (1,8,9).
\]

In particular B({2,3})=9, and there are seven ordered triples.

**Proof.** From a+b=c and gcd(a,b)=1, all three pairs of entries are
coprime. Hence each of the primes 2 and 3 can occur in only one entry.
At least one entry is therefore 1. It cannot be c. After swapping a,b
we may set a=1. If b=1 then c=2. Otherwise b and c exceed 1, so their
nonempty prime supports are disjoint. They are consequently a power of
2 and a power of 3, in some order.

First suppose 2^m-3^n=1, with m,n positive integers. Modulo 3, m is even,
say m=2k. Then

\[
(2^k-1)(2^k+1)=3^n.
\]

The two positive odd factors have gcd one. Since their product is a power
of 3, one of them is 1. The smaller is therefore 1, giving k=1 and n=1.
This produces (1,3,4).

Next suppose 3^n-2^m=1. If m=1, then n=1, giving (1,2,3). If m is at
least 2, reduction modulo 4 shows that n is even, say n=2k. Thus

\[
(3^k-1)(3^k+1)=2^m.
\]

Both factors are powers of 2 and differ by 2. Their gcd is 2, so the
smaller factor must be 2 and the larger 4. Hence k=1 and m=3. This gives
(1,8,9). Direct substitution verifies all four listed triples. No
Catalan-type theorem is used. \(\square\)

**Corollary 4.** For every epsilon>0 and every triple in this sector,

\[
\log c\leq (1+\epsilon)\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)
+\max\{0,\log 9-(1+\epsilon)\log 6\}.
\]

Indeed the first triple has radical 2 and the other three radical 6.
Theorem 3 controls all exponents in this sector, without a numerical
search cutoff. It is an elementary known special case, not a newly claimed
ABC exponent or a historical novelty claim.

For the actual Komorebi view system in Proposition 2 we may now use B=9.
At N=4 the modulus is 24. Since 24 exceeds 2B and contains both primes
in S, every congruent lift is already an exact primitive triple. Every
restriction above level 4 is therefore a bijection, and the coherent
section set consists of the seven declared source points. This stabilization
uses the proved arithmetic budget, rather than assuming one from local
compactness.

## 4. The uniform leaf that remains

Write R(S)=the product of primes in S, with R(empty)=1. Proposition 1
establishes the existence of B(S), separately for each finite S. Ordinary
ABC is equivalent to the following additional uniform estimate:

\[
\text{for every }\epsilon>0,\quad
\sup_{S\text{ finite}}
\{\log B(S)-(1+\epsilon)\log R(S)\}<\infty.
\tag{U}
\]

To check the forward implication, apply ABC to every point of T_S and
use rad(abc) at most R(S). Empty source sets have B(S)=1. Conversely,
for any primitive positive additive triple use its actual support set S;
then c is at most B(S) and R(S)=rad(abc). A nonnegative constant suffices
in both directions. Thus (U) isolates the exact remaining quantitative
claim. Its equivalence to the original target is a reduction, not a proof
of that claim.

A cardinality bound alone cannot bound the location of points: singleton
sets of positive integers have arbitrarily large maxima. This observation
is a limitation of that inference, not a counterexample to (U) on its
arithmetic source sets. A new argument must use the exact additive coupling
of discrete prime powers to obtain growth control on B(S).

## 5. Outcome and continuation

The fixed-support source is finite by a checked external theorem, and its
bounded Komorebi realization follows with a budget known to exist. The
two-prime sector has a complete elementary proof, an explicit budget 9,
finite stabilization and an explicit restricted ABC constant. No new Lean
compilation or load-bearing numerical computation was performed.

The next quantitative continuation is to prove a useful bound on B(S) as
the support varies, by an arithmetic argument rather than by counting the
number of solutions. Adding a new prime to the elementary sector changes
the allocation patterns: all three entries may exceed 1, with different
primes assigned to the three pairwise coprime entries. The two-factor
argument above no longer enumerates all cases. The route remains open at this
specific growth bound. No impossibility conclusion is drawn from the
absence of such a bound in the imported counting theorem.
