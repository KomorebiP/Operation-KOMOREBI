ABSTRACT
A generating family is meaningful only relative to a specified operator algebra. We develop a common framework for
generation and reconstruction in which a module is equipped with a fixed operator grammar, relations, and a finite
family of pairwise annihilating idempotents. The projectors give exact analysis and synthesis maps, while the blocks
𝑃𝛽𝑇𝑃𝛼encode operator composition and transfer between packet spaces. We study the minimum number of module
generators, called seed rank relative to the grammar, and its behavior under quotients, direct sums, tensor products,
coordinate changes, and intertwining maps.
For a supplied split semisimple presentation, the generator count is max𝑖⌈𝑚𝑖/𝑑𝑖⌉. For a single operator with explicit
primary data, it is the largest certified block multiplicity. These formulas are proved from the stated decomposition and
quotient data, not from new classification theorems. Bounded projectors extend the coordinate calculus to normed
spaces and completions. Worked models include a quadratic coordinate algebra, a bilateral shift, matrix modules, a
typed quiver, and path and cycle adjacency operators.
The accompanying Lean development checks 35 registered claims at their stated scope, including three mathematical
algorithm specifications. The source-to-statement map separates these results from the classical existence theorems used
to supply their hypotheses and from the finite computations in the Python companion.
Keywords. Module generators; idempotent decompositions; block operators; cyclic vectors; formal verification.

1 The foundational problem
1.1 Generation relative to a fixed grammar
Fix a unital operator algebra A acting on a vector space 𝑋. The question whether a family 𝑆generates 𝑋depends on this
action: the same vector may be cyclic for one algebra and fail to be cyclic for another. Choosing an operator separately
for each target would conceal rather than solve the generation problem. The operator grammar must therefore be
specified before the target is chosen.
Our aim is to keep the generating action and a decomposition of 𝑋in the same description. A finite family of
idempotents gives packet coordinates; each operator then has a table of packet-to-packet blocks. Module generators
answer which vectors can be reached, and the blocks describe how the operators move between the chosen components.
Relations and topology enter when the realization is a quotient or a completion.
The resulting language combines standard module theory with explicit coordinate and transport identities. The
term seed rank records the minimum number of generators relative to the declared action. It is not a replacement for
the classical module invariant. The contribution here is to develop these ingredients together, with proofs, worked
realizations, and a precise correspondence to a formal library.

1.3 Scope and conventions
The core is linear. Packet families are finite unless a mode of convergence is specified, and pairwise annihilation does not
mean orthogonality for an inner product. Normed-space statements use bounded linear operators over a nontrivially
normed field; the Hilbert-space statements use R or C. Unless stated otherwise, the algebraic ground ring is a field k.
For the semiring-valued generator inequalities, a generated hull means the subsemimodule spanned by the seed family.
Classification results enter only through explicit data. In particular, a chosen split algebra and module equivalence,
primary generators, polynomial projectors, scalar quotient maps, or an eigenbasis must be supplied where the theorem
requires them. Their existence is a separate classical input. The formal development does not include a general Jordan
or Wedderburn construction, an exact eigensolver, or the projective geometry of the coordinate-ring example. These
boundaries are recorded again, claim by claim, in the verification ledger.

3.3 Algebra generation and module generation
Two notions must not be confused.
• An algebra may require several algebra generators.
• The same algebra, regarded as a module over itself, is generated by its unit.
For example, k[𝑥, 𝑦] requires the two algebra generators 𝑥and 𝑦, but it has module seed rank one under the
multiplication algebra because every polynomial is obtained by applying a multiplication operator to 1.
This distinction will recur throughout the series. A small module seed does not imply that the operator grammar is
small; both quantities must be recorded.

17 Classical inputs, new organization, and claim boundary
17.1 Classical mathematical inputs
The following are established ingredients:
• modules over associative algebras and path algebras;
• cyclic modules and companion matrices;
• Jordan decomposition and primary decomposition;
• Wedderburn’s structure theorem for finite-dimensional semisimple algebras;
• direct-sum and tensor-product constructions;
• idempotent decompositions and block matrices;
• spectral theory of symmetric graph adjacency matrices.
These facts are not renamed as new theorems. In the kernel-checked statements, their outputs appear as explicit
inputs: a chosen split algebra equivalence, a compatible module equivalence, primary block generators, extraction
polynomials, scalar quotient maps, an eigenbasis, or exact spectrum-coverage data. Lean verifies the conclusions from
those certificates. It does not, in this release, derive the certificates from the classification theorems themselves. This
distinction is part of the formal claim boundary rather than a hidden implementation detail.

The organizing choice is to keep the acting algebra, generated module, relations, projector coordinates, and realization

18 Conclusion
A fixed operator algebra turns a generation claim into a question about a module. A finite projector family gives exact
coordinates on that module, and the transfer blocks recover both the operator and its composition law. Relations
determine the presentation; topology determines which closures or completions are justified.
The multiplicity formulas show why these distinctions matter. A cyclic component may require several generators
when it is repeated under a shared action. A path has an endpoint Krylov basis, whereas a complex cycle has a two-
dimensional scalar quotient that prevents one-seed generation. The number of seeds is therefore a property of the action,
not of a preferred coordinate description.
The results collected here provide a common starting point for subsequent realizations: a specified grammar, a
generated module, a minimum generator count, packet coordinates, and exact transport identities. The formal record
preserves the assumptions on which those statements depend. Extensions can build on this core without confusing a
chosen decomposition with a proof that such a decomposition exists.
